# Design notes

Heist! started from one idea: two teams, a town made of tiles, robbers working together on a secret target, and cops who must agree unanimously on what it is. Everything else grew out of brainstorming around that. The original documents are in [design-history](design-history/).

## Goals

- **A family euro-style game** that a smart 12-year-old can play with adults. Younger players can play Break-In.
- **Tense and strategic.** Both teams plan at the same time, so every night is a guessing game about the other side's guess.
- **About an hour.** 10 nights, with a race to the finish rather than a points count.
- **Different every time.** A modular town changes which jobs are possible and how far everything is from everything else.

## Key decisions

| Decision | Why |
|---|---|
| **Simultaneous planning, then reveal** | Keeps both teams involved every moment and creates the "I know that you know" tension. |
| **Pawns move openly on the board** | More fun and tangible than a secret map. Face-down destination tokens and 2 blank decoys per team keep the intent hidden. |
| **Overlapping item recipes** | Every item fits more than one target, so robbers can't avoid leaving clues, only blur them. This is the whole deduction engine. |
| **A race win** | Robbers win with a heist; cops win with a correct unanimous accusation or at dawn. No points system and no "lockdown and scramble" phase, which would push the game long and blunt the finale. |
| **Spent action cards as standard** | A shared team hand that refills at shift change. The used piles are public, so *when* you spend a card becomes strategy, and an empty Chase pile is a clue. Free-hand play is the beginner mode (Break-In). |
| **Double agents in layers** | Loyalty cards and Internal Affairs at 4+ players; Bribe and Snitch arrive as Shady Deal event cards; a fixed number of Double Agent cards so a team can't be taken over. None in 1v1. |
| **Internal Affairs** | Cops must accuse unanimously, so a mole could block every vote. Internal Affairs lets a team expose a suspected traitor, at a price if they're wrong. |
| **Lone Wolf** | A last gasp after the cops win, where one robber can win alone. It gives robbers a reason to hoard items personally, which makes the gang a little less trusting. |
| **Rules in five modes** | Learn one layer at a time, like a criminal career: Petty Theft, Break-In, The Big Job, Inside Job, Lone Wolf. |
| **3-pawn crews at every count** | The game plays the same at 2, 4 and 6 players; only how the pawns are shared out changes. |

## The town

Every tile is one city block with half a street on each side, a half crosswalk at the middle of each edge and a street lamp at each corner. All four edges are the same, so any layout works and tiles never rotate (the oblique view has a front). The Hardware Store is always in because it supplies two items for three targets; without it, the cops' job gets far too easy.

## Art direction

1940s–50s film noir, drawn like a movie set seen from above at an angle: black and grey buildings, sodium-lamp amber light pools, the odd red neon sign. Titles use the Limelight typeface. The rulebook cover follows the layout of a vintage film poster, using the game's own characters.

## Originality

The theme and some mechanisms sit near other games: city chase and deduction (Scotland Yard, Letters from Whitechapel), tile-based heists (Burgle Bros), hidden traitors (The Resistance, Shadows over Camelot), and the cops-and-robbers theme itself (Diabolik: Heists & Investigations). What looks new is the combination: open movement with hidden intent, "the prep leaks the plan" overlapping item recipes, a unanimous accusation paired with a possible mole, two-way double agents, and the Lone Wolf finish. The rules text and art are original.
